Abstract
Large foundation models have accelerated progress toward general-purpose agents
that interact with humans and other agents through language and multimodal signals.
However, robust multi-agent decision-making requires reasoning about what other
agents know, intend, and are likely to do under partial observability. Current agentic
systems often operate through prompt design, memory, or end-to-end behavioral
shaping, but typically do not learn an explicit partner-state representation that
can be reused as a decision variable across tasks. We introducemental-model-
enabled agents, a framework that equips an agent with a latent mental model of
its counterpart, allowing it to infer hidden beliefs, intentions, and likely reactions
from the observed history and use these inferences to guide action selection. Our
method learns an amortized recursive Theory-of-Mind representation, with first-
and second-order mental-state structure, jointly with a belief-conditioned reward
model that evaluates candidate actions relative to the inferred partner state. A
policy is then learned under this belief-aware signal, yielding an agent that can
act independently at inference time while retaining the benefits of explicit partner
modeling. We evaluate the same framework on both language-only and multimodal
benchmarks. Across these settings, explicit mental-state modeling consistently
improves interaction quality and Theory-of-Mind performance over base agentic
systems, showing that structured partner modeling is a useful inductive bias for

1 Introduction
Modern AI, robotics, and cognitive science increasingly converge on a shared objective: building
agents that can reason, plan, and act effectively in the world. The core idea is that intelligent systems,
both biological and artificial, must construct “mental models” of the world. This is in line with
Kenneth Craik’s seminal workThe Nature of Explanation, which proposed that organisms maintain
small-scale internal representations of external reality [3]. Such mental models allow them to simulate
possible actions, anticipate outcomes, and adapt effectively to changing environments. However, in
interactive environments, the world is partly constituted by other agents whose goals, beliefs, and
strategies are not directly observable. As a result, successful action requires modeling not only the
environment, but also other minds, a capacity commonly studied as Theory of Mind (ToM) [8].
In humans, ToM is mediated by compact representations of a partner’s latent mental states that support
prediction and planning [8]. This perspective has motivated computational approaches that learn agent
models directly from behavioral traces. ToMnet [22], for instance, showed that an observer can infer
latent agent properties from past trajectories and use them to predict future behavior, demonstrating
the value of explicit uncertainty over unobserved mental state and amortized inference that adapts
rapidly to novel agents. These properties are particularly valuable in multi-agent settings, where
partial observability is fundamental.
Large foundation models have accelerated progress toward general-purpose agents that interact with
humans and other agents through language and multimodal signals. Yet robust interactive competence
remains difficult. An agent must infer what other agents know, want, and are likely to do, and
choose actions that improve outcomes under partial observability (Fig.1). Depending on the setting,
this may involve cooperation, negotiation, assistance, competition, or adherence to conventions and
norms. Empirically, despite strong conversational ability, current LLMs lag behind humans on core
ingredients of interactive reasoning, including Theory-of-Mind-style inference [23, 29, 26], adherence
to interaction norms [ 31], and robust performance across goal-driven multi-agent scenarios [ 36].
Humans succeed in such settings because they rapidly form and update beliefs about others from
limited evidence [13, 28]. By contrast, current LLM agents typically do not maintain an explicit
model of a partner’s hidden goals, beliefs, or strategies. As a result, they may misread intent, commit
to brittle plans, and struggle to adapt when the interaction unfolds in unexpected ways.
Most prior work on LLM-based multi-agent systems improves behavior through shaping or scaffold-
ing rather than explicit partner-state estimation. Existing approaches include instruction tuning and
preference-based reinforcement learning that steer generation [19, 25, 30], prompt-level mechanisms
such as self-critique, reflection, and memory that condition the agent on textual summaries of prior
interaction [1, 20], and multi-turn synthetic self-play that induces complex interaction skills such as
consensus-building [18]. Across these approaches, partner dynamics are represented only implicitly
either in the prompt text or trajectory summaries and not embedded in policy’s latent dynamics. The
result is the absence of an explicit belief state with clear semantics and quantified uncertainty, which
limits interpretability, complicates reuse across tasks, and prevents partner modeling from being
integrated systematically into different policy learners.
A long line of work in sequential decision-making under partial observability suggests that effective
multi-agent behavior depends on maintaining beliefs over hidden state, including other agents’
objectives, information states, and internal models [12, 5, 6]. Extending this principle to foundation-
model agents is nontrivial. When beliefs about a partner are expressed only as short natural-language
hypotheses or serialized summaries appended to prompts, they remain limited in expressivity, and
downstream action selection becomes tightly coupled to prompt formulation. This motivates the
central question of our work:can an agent learn an amortized latent belief state from interaction
history and use it as a stable training signal for multi-agent policy learning?
In this paper, we address this question withmental-model-enabled agentsfor multi-agent systems
under partial observability. Our approach learns a compact latent belief state from interaction history
that captures hidden variables of other agents, including goals, beliefs, knowledge, strategies, and
higher-order expectations. We then learn a reward model that evaluates candidate actions relative to
this inferred state, rather than only the observable context. The resulting belief-conditioned reward is

To validate the generality of our method, we evaluate it on both language-only and multimodal
benchmarks for multi-agent systems. For language-only settings, we consider SOTOPIA [ 36],
CRAIGSLIST-BARGAIN[ 9], TOMI[ 15], and BIGTOM [ 7]; for multimodal evaluation, we use
MMROLE[ 4]. We also convert existing resources into ToM-supervised training data by augmenting
them with mental-state annotations; Appendix Table 10 summarizes the additional supervision
relative to the original releases. Across these benchmarks, explicit mental-state modeling consistently
improves downstream performance. For example, on SOTOPIA, our approach with Qwen2.5-7B
achieves an average relative gain of 11.5% over the base model across two partner settings, while on
BIGTOM it improves paired TB/FB accuracy by 26.7 pp on average across the six reported settings.
In summary, our contributions are:
• We propose a general framework for ToM-guided multi-agent systems under partial ob-
servability, in which an agent learns an explicit latent belief state over other agents from
observed history and uses it to support downstream decision making.
• We introduce a recursive mental-state architecture and a coupled mental-state/reward learn-
ing objective that make first- and second-order partner representations directly useful for
evaluating candidate actions.
• We show how the learned mental signal can be distilled into standalone policies, so that
explicit partner modeling improves training while deployment remains efficient.
• We evaluate the framework across language-only and multimodal benchmarks, and aug-
ment these resources with additional ToM supervision, utility signals, rationales, and hard
negatives to support training and analysis.
2 Related Work
Multi-agent systems.Recent work has explored LLM-based multi-agent systems in which multiple
agents coordinate through role specialization, dialogue, memory, and tool use. CAMEL studies
cooperative role-conditioned interactions in an agent society setting [16]. MetaGPT, ChatDev, and
AutoGen emphasize structured workflows, standardized operating procedures, and programmable
conversation patterns for collaborative problem solving [10, 21, 32]. These systems improve perfor-
mance mainly through external orchestration, such as role decomposition, protocol design, and tool
integration. Our work is complementary: rather than modifying only the interaction protocol, we
focus on internal partner modeling under partial observability by learning a latent belief state and
using it to shape downstream control.
Interactive environments and agent training.Another line of work develops environments and
training pipelines for interactive agents. SOTOPIA[ 36] provides open-ended, multi-turn interactions
with private goals and multidimensional evaluation, and subsequent work studies imitation and
self-reinforcement, utterance-level reward design, and scalable simulation in this setting [36, 30, 34,
35]. In the multimodal setting, MMROLEintroduces a framework for developing and evaluating
multimodal role-playing agents using persona-grounded images, dialogues, and reward-model-based
evaluation [4]. These works provide important testbeds and training recipes for interactive agents, but
they generally do not learn an explicit amortized partner model that is reused as a decision variable
across benchmarks. Our approach instead learns a latent partner representation from interaction
history, couples it with reward modeling, and distills the resulting signal into a policy.
Theory of Mind and agent modeling.ToM aims to infer or represent agents’ latent mental states
(beliefs, goals, intentions) to predict future behavior and mental states. ToMnet [ 22] learns latent
models of agents from behavioral traces to predict future actions and agent-specific characteristics. In
language-only settings, ToMi, BigToM, ToMBench, and OpenToM provide increasingly controlled or
comprehensive evaluations of belief tracking and mental-state reasoning [15, 7, 2, 33]. SymbolicToM
shows that explicit belief tracking can substantially improve ToM reasoning at decoding time [24].
In multimodal settings, MMToM-QA and MuMA-ToM extend ToM evaluation beyond text, and
MuMA-ToM further introduces LIMP, a model-based inverse multi-agent planning approach for
multimodal mental-state inference [11, 27]. Relative to this literature, our contribution is not a new

3 Methodology
We study multi-agent interaction under partial observability. As shown in Fig. 2, we first learn an
amortized latent mental-model of a counterpart’s hidden state together with a utility model over
candidate actions. We then use this learned signal to train a standalone policy. Finally, we deploy the
policy directly at test time. We present the detailed mathematical formulation below. For notational
clarity, we present the two-agent case with one acting agent and one counterpart.
3.1 Amortized Mental-model Learning
We consider sequential interaction over a finite horizon T . At time t, the acting agent receives an
observation ot, maintains the interaction history ht = (o1, a1, . . . , ot), and selects an action at ∈ A.
The action may be free-form language or a structured environment action. In multimodal settings, ot
may also contain visual or other non-textual signals.
The effect of at depends not only on the external environment, but also on latent mental properties of
the counterpart, such as goals, beliefs, knowledge, capabilities, and strategies. We denote these hidden
variables by zt. A Bayes-optimal agent would maintain the posterior p(zt |h t), but exact inference is
generally intractable in open-ended interaction. We therefore learn an amortized mental-state model
qω(zt |h t)≈p(z t |h t),(1)
which maps the observable history to a compact latent belief state.
However, ToM is inherently multi-order (as illustrated in Figure 4d). It requires reasoning not only
about a partner’s first-order state, but also about that partner’s model of agent or environment. We
capture this recursion by factorizing the mental-model latent as
qω(z(1)t , z(2)t |h t) =q ω1 (z(1)t |h t)q ω2 (z(2)t |h t, z(1)t ).(2)
Here z(1)t represents the first-order partner state, while z(2)t represents higher-order state conditioned
on the first-order inference. Each mental-latent z(ℓ)t is decomposed into semantically meaningful
subspaces representing belief, intent and thought-process, which are the fundamental elements of
human theory-of-mind

3.2 Mental-Model-Aware Utility
A latent mental-model on its own cannot act as a decision variable and therefore cannot inform the
agent what to do. To translate mental-model into action, we need a way to evaluate candidate actions
against the inferred counterpart state. We therefore learn a reward utility function that scores arbitrary
candidates conditioned on the mental-model latent.
For an evaluation setting e, let Ue(ht, at, θt)∈R De be the outcome vector for action at under the
hidden counterpart state, and let we ∈∆ De−1 be the scalarization of those dimensions. For example,
an open-ended social setting may score goal completion and social quality, while a single-step ToM
setting may score answer correctness. We learn a mental-model-conditioned utility predictor
¯uω,ψ (ht, at) =E zt∼qω(·|ht) [ˆuψ(ht, at, zt)].(7)
where ˆuψ(ht, at, zt) is the outcome from the utility head Rψ. This expectation turns the latent
mental model into a score over candidate actions. It asks how good action at is on average across the
mental states that remain plausible given the history. The supervision used to fit Rψ varies across
benchmarks. Refer to Appendix A.1 for a detailed explanation.
Learning the mental-model and the utility separately tends to produce two complementary failure
modes. A mental-model trained only on mental-state annotations becomes interpretable but decision-
irrelevant. It captures what is describable about the partner rather than what is actionable. A reward
model trained without pressure to use that latent explains its scores through surface features of the
candidate response and ignores partner state entirely. We therefore optimize the two jointly, so that
gradients from the reward objective shape the mental-model toward decision-relevance, and the
mental-model in turn forces the reward model to route through it.

4 Experimentation
Implementation details.All trained open-source agents use LoRA adapters on 7B-scale language
or vision-language backbones. We use Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct as the primary language backbone,
including LLaMA2-7B-Chat and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 as additional SOTOPIAcomparisons,
and use Qwen2.5-VL-Chat for multimodal experiments on MMRole. For SOTOPIA, we augment
SOTOPIA- π[30] episodes with ToM labels and utility annotations to train the mental-state/reward
model; for BigToM and ToMi, we train on 5K synthetic BigToM-style samples and evaluate on the
official BIGTOM and TOMIsplits; for MMROLE, we augment the released training set with mental
labels. CRAIGSLIST-BARGAINand FANTOM are used only as transfer evaluations. We first train the
mental-state/reward model and then optimize the policy with supervised warmup when used, followed
by mental-reward-guided GRPO using rewards from the frozen scorer. Detailed data construction,
sample counts, latent dimensions, optimizer settings, decoding parameters, and hardware are reported
in Appendix A.1.3; Appendix Table 10 summarizes the augmented supervision, and Appendix A.2.4
reports automated filtering and blinded human verification.
Baselines and evaluation.For SOTOPIA, we compare against zero-shot/base LLaMA2-7B, Mistral-
7B, and Qwen2.5-7B agents, SFT and GRPO variants trained on our generated data, and closed-source
GPT-4o-mini/Gemini baselines, evaluating with the official episode-level seven-dimension SOTOPIA
scoring protocol on all, hard, and partner-averaged settings. Craigslist-Bargain uses the released
negotiation protocol and reports deal, walkaway, and no-agreement rates. For BigToM and ToMi,
we follow the official evaluation scripts and report the released TB/FB/TB∧FB, Average, Joint, and
false-belief split metrics against GPT-style and classic ToMi baselines where available. For MMRole,
we compare with the official closed-source/open-source VLM and MMRole-Agent baselines and
report the benchmark’s normalized role-playing dimensions and overall score.

4.1 Main Experimentation Results
Results on SOTOPIA.Table 1 reports the main SOTOPIA-ALLevaluation results under a fixed GPT-
4o-mini partner. Our method improves the overall score for all three open-source agents (LLaMA:
3.330→3.541, Mistral: 3.511→3.784, Qwen: 3.290→3.856), indicating that the gains are not specific
to one model family. The pronounced improvements on task-facing dimensions such as Knowledge
and Goal, with high and stable Believability, suggests that the learned mental-model signal improves
goal-directed reasoning without making the agents less plausible. Table 2 shows the same pattern on
SOTOPIA-HARD, where average scores improve for all three LLM agents, showing that the method
continues to help when scenarios demand more difficult partner modeling.
Table 3 evaluates generalization across partner types by averaging results over the GPT-4o-mini and
self-partner settings. The improvement is consistent across all agents (LLaMA: 6.588→7.245 ,
Mistral: 7.109→7.552 , and Qwen: 7.087→7.633 ). These gains suggest that the method does
not depend on a particular partner model and transfers across both closed-source and open-source
counterparts.
Figure 3 summarizes the aggregate SOTOPIAtrends. Our 7B Qwen2.5 agent with explicit mental-
model training outperforms stronger closed-source baselines in the top panel, while the bottom panel
shows consistent gains over SFT-only and GRPO-only variants (trained on our ToM-augmented
data) averaged across LLaMA, Mistral, and Qwen. This suggests that explicit partner-state modeling
contributes beyond standard supervised or reward-only training.
Finally, Table 4 evaluates cross-domain transfer. We evaluate the agents trained exclusively with
SOTOPIA-derived ToM data on a random subset of 100 CRAIGSLISTBARGAINnegotiation examples
without any task-specific fine-tuning. The models retain high deal rates and reduce walkaways.
Together, Tables 1–4 and Figure 3 suggest that explicit partner-state modeling improves both in-
domain social interaction and out-of-domain negotiation transfer.
Results on BIGTOM & TOMI.Tables 5 & 7 evaluate transfer from open-ended dialogue to
controlled ToM reasoning. BIGTOM (Table 5) is especially diagnostic because TB ∧FB only rewards
a model when both branches of the same scenario are correct. The model must maintain two
counterfactual mental states for the same agent. Base models often handle the easier true-belief
branch but lose consistency once the false-belief branch or initial belief changes. SFT on our enriched
data improves, but it still treats the output largely as a supervised answer pattern. In contrast, our
mental-model variant is more consistent across forward belief, forward action, and backward belief
because of its ability to encode higher order hidden states (further comparisons Appendix Table 13).

the BIGTOM training format. We cross-evaluate our policy trained with BIGTOM-style data on

6 Conclusion
We introduced a mental-model-enabled agent framework for multi-agent systems, where an agent
learns an amortized recursive Theory-of-Mind latent state and couples it to a belief-conditioned
reward model. The resulting reward signal is used to train policies that act independently at test time
while retaining the benefits of explicit partner-state modeling. Across SOTOPIA, negotiation transfer,
BIGTOM/TOMI/FANTOM, and MMROLE, the same framework improves open-source agents on
social-interaction and Theory-of-Mind benchmarks. Our results also show that augmenting existing
datasets with mental-state annotations, utility signals, rationales, and contrastive negatives provides
useful supervision for learning reusable partner representations.
Limitations and future work.Our training environments are primarily dyadic; the zero-shot
FANTOM transfer in Appendix A.3 provides initial multi-party evidence, while end-to-end multi-
party training with participant-specific latent updates remains future work. The same formulation can
also be extended beyond language and image-grounded role play to embodied and robotic settings,
where partner beliefs, goals, visibility, and physical constraints evolve over longer horizons. Finally,
scaling to larger policies, more diverse partner models, richer online belief updates, and human-in-the-
loop evaluation would help test how far explicit mental-state structure continues to improve robust
multi-agent behavior.

A.1 Methodology, Architecture, and Training Details
A.1.1 Dataset-Specific Methodology and Notation
The main paper presents the benchmark-agnostic version of our method. Here we give the concrete
instantiations used for each dataset. Across all benchmarks, an example is represented as a tuple
(ci, a+i , a−i ,u i,γ i, m(1)i , m(2)i , ξi),(12)
where ci is the observable context, a+i is the observed or preferred response, a−i is an optional
hard negative, ui is a dataset-specific utility vector, γi is an optional dimension mask indicating
which utility dimensions a contrastive negative is intended to violate, m(1)i and m(2)i are first- and
second-order mental-state targets when available, and ξi is optional rationale or reward-explanation
text. Missing fields are handled by masking the corresponding auxiliary losses, which lets the same
objective support open-ended dialogue, structured belief QA, and multimodal role-play.
A.1.2 Architecture Overview
Stage 1 uses a single causal language-model or vision-language backbone with low-rank adapters
and task-specific heads. The backbone encodes the task context ci and, when scoring a candidate
action, the isolated action ai in separate forward passes, producing pooled context and action states
that feed the latent posterior and reward heads. In our implementation, both recursive latent levels use
dz = 128 dimensions and are partitioned into belief, intent, and thought subspaces. These subspaces
are not required by the general objective, but they provide a stable interface for attaching first-order
and second-order mental-state decoders.
A.1.3 Implementation Details
All open-source models are trained with LoRA adapters on 7B-scale backbones. For language-only
experiments, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct is the main policy and reward backbone; LLaMA2-7B-Chat
and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 are included for additional SOTOPIAcomparisons. For multimodal
experiments, we use Qwen2.5-VL-Chat. The training sources are: SOTOPIA-πepisodes augmented
with ToM labels and utility annotations, 5K synthetic BIGTOM-style scenarios with first- and
second-order mental labels, and MMROLEinter-role training turns augmented with visual ToM
labels. TOMI, official BIGTOM, official SOTOPIA, CRAIGSLIST-BARGAIN, and FANTOM are used
only for evaluation or transfer checks. SOTOPIAtraining episodes do not overlap with the original
SOTOPIAevaluation episodes.
Across benchmarks, we optionally use supervised warmup or mental-prefix SFT before GRPO. Unless
otherwise noted, the latent mental state uses dz = 128 with belief, intent, and thought subspaces.
Training uses bf16 mixed precision, AdamW, warmup scheduling, KL annealing, and delayed
second-order latent updates. Typical hyperparameter ranges are LoRA rank 8–32, LoRA α= 16 –64,
dropout 0.05, reward-model learning rate 2×10−5, GRPO learning rate 1×10−6–3×10−6, group size
8, clipping coefficient 0.1–0.2, KL coefficient 0.06–0.1, temperature 0.6–0.8, and top-p0.9 –0.95.
Experiments were run on NVIDIA RTX A6000 48GB GPUs.

A.1.6 Deployment and Evaluation
At deployment time, only the learned policy is retained: the reward model is not used for test-time
search or reranking. For open-ended interaction spaces, the policy directly generates a natural-
language response. For structured action spaces, the same wrapper prompts the policy to emit a
format-constrained output and parses it into the benchmark’s expected action object. Episodes are
then executed in each benchmark’s native environment with the trained policy occupying one agent
slot and the fixed partner or evaluator specified by the benchmark protocol.
A.2 Dataset-Specific Instantiations
The shared objective is defined once in Appendix A.1.4. To separate methodological choices from
dataset bookkeeping, each subsection below follows the same order: data construction, training
instance, and policy/evaluation protocol. We specify only the context, utility target, mental-state
supervision, and contrastive signal that change across datasets rather than re-expanding the same loss
terms.
A.2.1 BIGTOM
Original dataset.BIGTOM [ 7] is a procedurally generated theory-of-mind benchmark of 200
scenarios. Each scenario is a five-sentence story with context, desire, perception cue, belief, and an
external causal event that changes the object’s state, paired with three questions (belief, desire, action)
and gold answers for both branches: the agentis awareof the causal event, oris not aware.
17

===== PAGE 18 =====
Augmentation.We expand the seed set 25 × by prompting Qwen3-235B with k=4 randomly
sampled seeds as in-context exemplars and the original 19-field BIGTOM schema. We sample 5,000
new scenarios at temperature 1.0 and retain only those passing a schema validator: exactly five
sentences, the fourth beginning with a belief statement, distinct aware/not-aware branches, and a
random event disjoint from the causal event. Generated scenarios are deduplicated against prior
outputs and preserved the original BIGTOM schema, allowing drop-in compatibility with downstream
tooling.
Because BIGTOM labels only first-order beliefs, we enrich each scenario with a second-order belief
by prompting Qwen3-235B in reasoning mode (temperature 0.4) to produce: (i) an inventedobserver,
a plausible second character physically present at the event; (ii) a paraphrase of the story; and (iii) for
each of the aware and not-aware conditions, a first-order belief (the agent’s belief about the object), a
second-order belief (the agent’s belief about what the observer believes about the object), and a 2–4
sentence chain-of-thought rationale ending in the predicted action. A validator rejects outputs with
empty fields or identical first-order beliefs across conditions.
Resulting training set.Each annotated synthetic scenario is flattened into two training exam-
ples, one per perceptual condition, yielding 9,964 rows from 5K generated BIGTOM-style scenar-
ios. Every row carries BIGTOM-compatible gold labels ( gold_belief, gold_action) alongside
our added fields (first_order_belief, second_order_belief, rationale, observer_name,
observer_role, paraphrase). This synthetic set is used for training; the official BIGTOM bench-
mark and TOMIare held out for evaluation. The rationale serves as the supervision target for
mental-state reasoning, and the second-order belief enables training and evaluation of higher-order
theory-of-mind beyond the scope of the original benchmark.
Training instance.For a scenario s, perceptual branch b∈ {aware,not_aware}, initial-belief
flag ι, and question q, the context is cBTi = [story ι(s);q] . The positive answer a+i =y(s, b, q)is
paired with the opposite-branch answer a−i =y(s, ¯b, q)as a hard negative. The scalar utility target
uBTi ∈ {0,1}denotes branch-consistent correctness, so this setting uses scalar reward regression
and an unmasked preference term. Generated first- and second-order beliefs supervise mental-state
reconstruction, while the rationale is included in the mental-reasoning target. The branch label is
used only by the optional aware/not-aware diagnostic classifier described above.
Optimization and evaluation.Policy learning uses mental-prefix SFT on the same QA prompts
followed by GRPO, with the frozen scalar action-conditioned utility head providing the response
reward and a reference-policy KL penalty. Prompt-level latent-only predictions are retained for
diagnostics rather than added to the group-relative advantage. Evaluation uses the official true-belief,
false-belief, and paired TB∧FB metrics.
A.2.2 SOTOPIA
Original dataset.SOTOPIA- π [30] builds on the SOTOPIAenvironment [ 35], which consists of
90 social scenarios and 40 character profiles. Each episode pairs two characters in a scenario and
assigns each a private social goal, covering negotiation, collaboration, and competition. Character
profiles include name, gender, occupation, personality description, and secrets. The released dataset
contains multi-turn social interaction episodes, each with a full conversation and episode-level scores
across seven social dimensions: believability, relationship, knowledge, secret-keeping, social rules,
financial benefits, and goal completion.
Augmentation.We apply a two-stage GPT-4o annotation pipeline to each episode, decomposing
each episode turn by turn and annotating every turn for both agents:
Stage A: reward scores with reasoning across all seven social dimensions, and first-order mental
state annotations – partner_belief (what the agent believes about the partner’s hidden intentions),
strategic_intent (the agent’s sub-goal for this specific turn), thought_process (the latent
reasoning connecting belief to action), and hard_negative_response (a fluent but socially poor
alternative response for contrastive learning).
Stage B: adds second-order theory-of-mind annotations for each agent-turn –
second_order_belief (what agent B thinks agent A believes), second_order_intent

(what agent B thinks agent A intends), and second_order_thought (what agent B thinks agent A
is thinking).
Resulting training set.In addition to the original SOTOPIA- π conversation episode context (sce-
nario, agent profiles, goals, secrets, and conversation), we generated per-turn reward scores with
reasoning across all seven dimensions for both agents, alongside first-order and second-order mental
state annotations, yielding 1,647 fully annotated episodes. These augmented SOTOPIA- π episodes are
used for training the mental-state/reward model; official SOTOPIAevaluation episodes are held out.

A.2.3 MMROLE
Original dataset.MMROLE[ 4] is a multimodal role-playing benchmark consisting of 85 charac-
ters, 11K images, and 14K single or multi-turn dialogues, yielding 85K training samples. Characters
span fictional figures (Marvel heroes, Harry Potter, Friends), real historical persons (Kobe Bryant,
Leonardo da Vinci), and Chinese historical figures. Each episode pairs a character profile (including
personality, life story, relationships, and catchphrases) with a real image from COCO or movie/TV
scripts. Three dialogue types are covered:comment(a character comments on an image),human-
role(a human and a character discuss an image), andinter-role(two characters interact about an
image). The original dataset evaluates role-playing quality across eight dimensions: coherency,
fluency, image-text relevance, instruction adherence, knowledge consistency, personality consistency,
response accuracy, and tone consistency.
Augmentation.We focus exclusively oninter-roledialogues and apply a three-step pipeline to add
visual theory-of-mind annotations.
Step 1 – Per-turn restructuring: Each inter-role dialogue is decomposed into per-turn snapshots.
Each turn contains the speaker and partner profiles, the image, the full dialogue history up to that
turn, and the current utterance, producing 36,744 per-turn examples.
Step 2 – Visual ToM annotation: Each per-turn example is annotated by GPT-4o with access to the
actual image, producing structured belief states:
• Visual percepts: objects in the scene and their salience (high/medium/low/none) for each
characterbased on their personality— a nurse and a politician attend to different elements
of the same image.
• First-order beliefs: what the speaker believes the partner is visually attending to and
intending.
19

===== PAGE 20 =====
•Second-order beliefs: what the speaker believes the partner believes about the speaker.
• Belief probes: structured question-answer pairs across five types (visual percept, first-order
belief, second-order belief, intent inference, false belief) at three difficulty levels.
•Contrastive belief states: a ToM-violating alternative response for preference learning.
Step 3 – Validation: Annotations are quality-filtered across six checks — object count, salience asym-
metry between agents, belief-scene grounding, probe answer distinctiveness, contrastive response
distinctiveness, and second-order belief consistency — producing a clean set of 15,607 annotated
turns.
Resulting training set.The 15,607 annotated turns are converted into four training formats using a
character-stratified split (74 train characters, 14 held-out test characters):
• Belief prediction(12,762 train rows): given image, agent profiles, and dialogue history,
predict the full structured belief state.
• Preference pairs(50,952 train rows): ToM-aligned responses paired against four types of
violation, wrong visual attribution, wrong first-order belief, wrong second-order belief, and
no theory-of-mind baseline, yielding four contrastive pairs per example.
• Probe QA(63,810 train rows): direct question-answer pairs for ToM evaluation and reward
model fine-tuning.
• Salience prediction(25,524 train rows): given image and one character profile, predict
per-object salience ratings.
Thus, MMROLEuses the released training dialogues as the base resource, augments them with visual
first-order/second-order mental labels and contrastive ToM violations, and evaluates on the held-out
role split under the official MMROLEprotocol.
Training instance.For MMROLE, the context is multimodal:
cMMi = Ii;ρ speakeri ;ρ partneri ;h i,<t; role_instruction i ,(34)

posteriors. The positive response is the current in-character utterance, while negatives are contrastive
ToM violations that alter visual grounding, first-order belief, second-order belief, or remove ToM

Optimization and evaluation.The multimodal reward model uses the shared utility-regression,

A.2.4 Mental-State Annotation and Human Verification
Mental-state annotations are generated from the source evidence available in each benchmark,
including the scenario or image, character profiles, dialogue history, and current turn. Before training,
every supervision set passes automated structural and semantic-consistency checks. On MMROLE,
the six-check validator retained 15,607/15,919 training turns (98.04%) and 291/294 examples from
the official test partition.
We additionally conducted a blinded human audit of100 randomly sampled annotations (50 each from
MMROLEand SOTOPIA). Twelve evaluators saw the underlying evidence and generated annotation,
but not the dataset or generator identity, and rated whether each mental-state claim was accurate and
supported on a five-point scale. The overall mean was 4.45/5 (89.0% after linear rescaling), with
90/100 annotations receiving at least 4 and 56 receiving 5. The lower-rated cases primarily involved
overconfident or unsupported inferences, underspecified claims, or contradictions with the dialogue
or image. Because the audit prioritizes coverage of distinct records rather than repeated ratings per
record, we treat it as a blinded quality spot-check rather than an inter-rater agreement study.

A.4 Impact Statement
This work studies explicit mental-model representations for multi-agent systems. The main positive
impact is to make agent behavior more interpretable and controllable in settings where an agent must
reason about another agent’s goals, beliefs, knowledge, or visual perspective. Such representations
can support safer assistants, better negotiation and collaboration agents, and clearer diagnostic
tools for evaluating whether a model is relying on task-relevant social and perceptual signals rather
than surface imitation. Our augmented datasets also provide structured supervision for studying
Theory-of-Mind behavior across language-only and multimodal benchmarks.
The same capability also carries risks. Models that infer or exploit another agent’s latent state could
be misused for manipulation, persuasion, privacy invasion, or profiling, especially if deployed in
high-stakes human-facing settings. The inferred variables in our framework should therefore be
treated as task-conditioned modeling constructs, not as ground-truth psychological states. They
may reflect annotation bias, dataset artifacts, or stereotypes in the underlying models and data. For
this reason, deployment should include consent-aware data collection, privacy filtering, bias audits,
uncertainty reporting, and restrictions against using inferred mental states for sensitive decisions
about individuals. Our experiments are limited to benchmark and synthetic/augmented training
settings, and future work should study human oversight and safety constraints before using such
systems in real-world multi-agent applications.
